1Histogram

Adjoining rectangles for a continuous frequency distribution; class boundaries on the x-axis.

Equal widths

Bar height = frequency

Unequal widths

Bar height = frequency density
Frequency density$$\text{FD} = \frac{\text{Class frequency}}{\text{Class width}}$$

The mode can be estimated graphically from the histogram.

2Ogives (cumulative frequency curves)

TypePlotShape
Less-thanLCF against upper class boundariesRises
Greater-thanGCF against lower class boundariesFalls

The two ogives intersect at the median. The median can also be read from the less-than ogive at $N/2$.

3Frequency polygon

Plot frequencies at class midpoints and join them with straight lines. Useful for comparing two distributions on one graph.

4Stem-and-leaf plot

Each value split into a stem (leading digits) and a leaf (trailing digit). Shows the shape and keeps every observation.

e.g. 107 → stem 10, leaf 7.

5Bar charts

  • Simple: one value per category
  • Component (sub-divided): parts of each category
  • Grouped (multiple): several values within each category

For categorical data, so bars are separated by gaps.

6Histogram vs bar chart

HistogramBar chart
DataContinuous quantitativeCategorical / discrete
BarsAdjacent, no gapsSeparated by gaps
AreaRepresents frequencyOnly height matters
OrderFixed by the scaleCategories can be reordered

7Pie chart

A circle split into sectors showing parts of a whole. The full circle is 360°.

Sector angle$$\frac{\text{Value of category}}{\text{Sum of all values}} \times 360^\circ$$

Best with a few categories that add up to a meaningful total.

8Boxplot (box-and-whisker)

Shows the five-number summary: minimum, $Q_1$, median (line inside the box), $Q_3$, maximum, plus the spread and potential outliers.

Interquartile range$$IQR = Q_3 - Q_1$$
Lower fence$$Q_1 - 1.5 \times IQR$$
Upper fence$$Q_3 + 1.5 \times IQR$$

Points beyond the fences are flagged as potential outliers.

!Common mistakes

  • Unequal class widths with raw frequencies as heights.
  • Plotting the less-than ogive at lower boundaries.
  • Leaving gaps between histogram bars.
  • Pie charts for data that are not parts of a whole.
