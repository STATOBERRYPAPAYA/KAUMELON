1What is statistics?

Statistics is the science which deals with the collection, organisation (classification), presentation, analysis and interpretation of data.

  • Origin: Italian statista (statesman), German Statistik, used by Gottfried Achenwall (1749).
  • Sir John Sinclair brought the word into English through the Statistical Account of Scotland (1791–99, 21 volumes).
  • Sir R.A. Fisher (1890–1962): Father of Modern Statistics. Gave us ANOVA, experimental design and maximum likelihood, and the principles of randomisation, replication and local control.

2Two branches

Statistics
DescriptiveSummarises a sample or population with measures such as the mean or standard deviation.
InferentialUses a random sample to describe and draw inferences about population parameters.

Data are individual pieces of factual information: the raw material of statistics (e.g. rainfall over a period, area under paddy in a state).

The error term captures the variability we cannot control: observation − true model mean.

4Population, sample, parameter

Population

The entire collection of units with the characteristic under study.
e.g. heights of all students in a college

Sample

A subset selected for study; should be representative of the population.
e.g. heights of 20 selected students

Parameter

A numerical characteristic of a population. It is fixed but usually unknown.
$\mu,\ \sigma^2,\ P$

Statistic

A value calculated from sample observations; used to estimate the parameter.
$\bar{x},\ s^2,\ \hat{p}$

7Collection of data

Primary data

First-hand, collected for the study's own purpose; no prior statistical treatment.
e.g. population census by the government

Secondary data

Already collected and processed by others for another purpose.
e.g. Economic Survey, Ministry reports

Primary methodspersonal investigation · trained enumerators · correspondents · phone or mailed questionnaire · online forms · mobile apps and social media polls

Secondary sourcesofficial (RBI, ministries, NSO) · institutional (universities, research institutes). Enumerator collects; respondent provides.

8Frequency distribution terms

Class limitsEnd points of a class, e.g. 110–119
Class boundariesClose the gaps: 109.5–119.5 for data recorded to the nearest cm
Continuous classesNo gaps (10–20, 20–30): limits = boundaries; use $10 \le x < 20$
Class markMid-point of the class interval
Class widthUpper boundary − lower boundary
LCF / GCFLess-than (at upper boundaries) / greater-than (at lower boundaries) running totals
Relative freq.$f/N$; all of them add up to 1

9Building a grouped table

  1. Decide the number of classes $k$
  2. Determine the class width
  3. Set up the class limits
  4. Tally items into classes (every 5th stroke crosses the first four)
  5. Count the frequency in each class

Raw data → ungrouped table (each value) or grouped table (classes). Grouping gives a clear picture but loses individual values.

11Formulae to remember

Number of classes (Sturges)$$k = 1 + 3.322\log_{10}N$$
Approximate class width$$C = \frac{\lvert \max - \min \rvert}{k}$$
Lower boundary of class $i$$$L_i = \frac{\text{LCL}_i + \text{UCL}_{i-1}}{2}$$
Class mark$$\frac{\text{Lower limit} + \text{Upper limit}}{2}$$
Relative frequency$$\text{RF} = \frac{f}{N}, \qquad \sum \frac{f}{N} = 1$$
Sum of frequencies$$\sum f = N$$

LCL / UCL = lower / upper class limit. Sturges' $k$ is a guide, not a compulsory number.

!Common mistakes

  • A large dataset cannot correct biased selection or poor measurement.
  • Association alone does not prove cause and effect.
  • Online forms and social media polls collect voluntary responses, so they are not automatically representative.
  • Classes must cover the data without overlap. Boundaries depend on the recording precision.
