1What is statistics?

Statistics is the science which deals with the collection, organisation (classification), presentation, analysis and interpretation of data.

  • Origin: Italian statista (statesman), German Statistik, used by Gottfried Achenwall (1749).
  • Sir John Sinclair brought the word into English through the Statistical Account of Scotland (1791–99, 21 volumes).
  • Sir R.A. Fisher (1890–1962): Father of Modern Statistics. Gave us ANOVA, experimental design and maximum likelihood, and the principles of randomisation, replication and local control.

2Two branches

Statistics
DescriptiveSummarises a sample or population with measures such as the mean or standard deviation.
InferentialUses a random sample to describe and draw inferences about population parameters.

Data are individual pieces of factual information: the raw material of statistics (e.g. rainfall over a period, area under paddy in a state).

3Statistics vs maths

MathematicsStatistics
ReasoningDeductiveInductive
ResultsExact, unchangingUnder uncertainty
HandlesAxioms, proofsVariable real-world data

The error term captures the variability we cannot control: observation − true model mean.

4Population, sample, parameter

Population

The entire collection of units with the characteristic under study.
e.g. heights of all students in a college

Sample

A subset selected for study; should be representative of the population.
e.g. heights of 20 selected students

Parameter

A numerical characteristic of a population. It is fixed but usually unknown.
$\mu,\ \sigma^2,\ P$

Statistic

A value calculated from sample observations; used to estimate the parameter.
$\bar{x},\ s^2,\ \hat{p}$

9Building a grouped table

  1. Decide the number of classes $k$
  2. Determine the class width
  3. Set up the class limits
  4. Tally items into classes (every 5th stroke crosses the first four)
  5. Count the frequency in each class

Raw data → ungrouped table (each value) or grouped table (classes). Grouping gives a clear picture but loses individual values.

!Common mistakes

  • Association alone does not prove cause and effect.
  • Online forms and social media polls collect voluntary responses, so they are not automatically representative.
  • Classes must cover the data without overlap. Boundaries depend on the recording precision.

3Crop Cutting Experiment (CCE): steps

A small, randomly located plot is harvested and weighed to estimate yield. Yield surveys for principal crops run under the General Crop Estimation Survey (GCES).

  1. Select the field: it must be larger than the CCE plot.
  2. Locate the plot from the south-west corner using random steps along the horizontal and vertical sides.
  3. Mark and harvest plants rooted inside the pegged, string-marked plot; apply the boundary rule consistently.
  4. Thresh on a clean mat and dry the grain.
  5. Winnow and weigh: subtract the empty bag to get the net grain weight.

4CCE numbers

Plot yield (kg/ha)$$\frac{10\,000\,w}{a}$$

$w$ = net grain (kg), $a$ = plot area (m²). 12.5 kg from 25 m² = 5000 kg/ha.

Plot shapes: 5×5 m, 10×10 m squares, 10×5 m rectangle; triangles (UP) and circles (WB) also documented.

7Agricultural Census

What

Structure of agriculture: number and area of operational holdings, land use, cropping pattern, inputs, down to village level.

When

Started 1970–71; quinquennial (every 5 years). 11th census: reference year 2021–22, launched 28 July 2022.

Land-record states: number and area by complete enumeration; characteristics on a sample basis.

1Histogram

Adjoining rectangles for a continuous frequency distribution; class boundaries on the x-axis.

Equal widths

Bar height = frequency

Unequal widths

Bar height = frequency density
Frequency density$$\text{FD} = \frac{\text{Class frequency}}{\text{Class width}}$$

The mode can be estimated graphically from the histogram.

2Ogives (cumulative frequency curves)

TypePlotShape
Less-thanLCF against upper class boundariesRises

The two ogives intersect at the median. The median can also be read from the less-than ogive at $N/2$.

3Frequency polygon

Plot frequencies at class midpoints and join them with straight lines. Useful for comparing two distributions on one graph.

4Stem-and-leaf plot

Each value split into a stem (leading digits) and a leaf (trailing digit). Shows the shape and keeps every observation.

e.g. 107 → stem 10, leaf 7.

5Bar charts

  • Simple: one value per category
  • Component (sub-divided): parts of each category
  • Grouped (multiple): several values within each category

For categorical data, so bars are separated by gaps.

7Pie chart

A circle split into sectors showing parts of a whole. The full circle is 360°.

Sector angle$$\frac{\text{Value of category}}{\text{Sum of all values}} \times 360^\circ$$

Best with a few categories that add up to a meaningful total.

8Boxplot (box-and-whisker)

Shows the five-number summary: minimum, $Q_1$, median (line inside the box), $Q_3$, maximum, plus the spread and potential outliers.

Interquartile range$$IQR = Q_3 - Q_1$$
Lower fence$$Q_1 - 1.5 \times IQR$$
Upper fence$$Q_3 + 1.5 \times IQR$$

Points beyond the fences are flagged as potential outliers.

  • Leaving gaps between histogram bars.
  • Pie charts for data that are not parts of a whole.

1The idea · types of averages

A measure of central tendency is a single value that represents the centre or typical value of a dataset.

Simple averages
this chapter		Mean · Median · Mode
Special averagesGeometric mean · Harmonic mean
Positional averagesQuartiles · Deciles · Percentiles

Objectives: condense data into a single value; make comparisons easy.

2A good average should be…

  • Rigidly defined
  • Simple to understand and easy to calculate
  • Based on all values of the data
  • Capable of further mathematical treatment
  • Stable from sample to sample (sampling stability)
  • Not unduly affected by extreme values

No single average meets all six. The mean fails only the last one.

3Arithmetic mean

The sum of the observations divided by their number. It is what people usually mean by "average".

Raw data$$\bar{x} = \frac{\sum x_i}{n}$$
Frequency distribution$$\bar{x} = \frac{\sum f_i x_i}{\sum f_i}$$
Assumed mean, $d_i = x_i - A$$$\bar{x} = A + \frac{\sum f_i d_i}{\sum f_i}$$
Coding, $u_i = (x_i - A)/C$$$\bar{x} = A + C\,\frac{\sum f_i u_i}{\sum f_i}$$
Weighted mean$$\bar{x}_w = \frac{\sum w_i x_i}{\sum w_i}$$
Combined mean of groups$$\bar{x} = \frac{\sum n_j \bar{x}_j}{\sum n_j}$$

For grouped data, $x_i$ is the class mark, so the mean is an approximation. With equal weights, the weighted mean is the simple mean.

4Properties of the mean

Deviations sum to zero$$\sum (x_i - \bar{x}) = 0$$
Least squares$$\sum (x_i - a)^2 \text{ is least at } a = \bar{x}$$
Change of origin & scale$$y_i = a + b x_i \Rightarrow \bar{y} = a + b\bar{x}$$

Combine group means by weighting with group sizes, not by averaging the means.

5Median

The middle value after arranging the data in ascending order. It is a positional average.

Odd $n$

$\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)^{\text{th}}$ observation

Even $n$

mean of the $\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)^{\text{th}}$ and $\left(\frac{n}{2}+1\right)^{\text{th}}$ observations
Grouped data (linear interpolation)$$Md = L + \left(\frac{\frac{N}{2} - F}{f_m}\right) C$$

Median class holds the $(N/2)^{\text{th}}$ value · $L$ its lower boundary · $F$ cumulative frequency below $L$ · $f_m$ its frequency · $C$ its width. Graphically, read it off the less-than ogive at $N/2$.

6Mode

The value that occurs most often. Found by inspection for raw data. It is the right average for qualitative (nominal) data.

Grouped data (equal class widths)$$Mo = L + \left(\frac{f_m - f_p}{2f_m - f_p - f_s}\right) C$$

Modal class has the highest frequency · $L$ its lower boundary · $f_p$, $f_s$ frequencies of the preceding and succeeding classes · $C$ class width. Graphically, use the histogram.

With unequal widths, compare frequency densities rather than raw frequencies. Data can be bimodal or have no mode.

8Shape & order

  • Symmetric, unimodal: mean = median = mode
  • Many positively skewed distributions: mean > median > mode
  • Many negatively skewed distributions: mean < median < mode

The skewed orderings are typical, not a universal theorem.

1Geometric mean (GM)

The $n$th root of the product of $n$ positive observations. Use it for growth rates, ratios, percentages and multiplicative change.

Definition$$GM = \sqrt[n]{\textstyle\prod x_i}$$
Using logs$$GM = \text{Antilog}\!\left(\frac{\sum \log x_i}{n}\right)$$
Frequency distribution$$GM = \text{Antilog}\!\left(\frac{\sum f_i \log x_i}{n}\right)$$

Needs positive values: a zero makes the product GM zero, and it is unsuitable for negatives.

2Harmonic mean (HM)

The reciprocal of the arithmetic mean of the reciprocals. Use it for rates, such as average speed over equal distances.

Raw data$$HM = \frac{n}{\sum \frac{1}{x_i}}$$
Frequency distribution$$HM = \frac{n}{\sum \frac{f_i}{x_i}}$$

GM and HM both give more weight to smaller values.

3AM · GM · HM

Together: the Pythagorean means.

Positive observations$$AM \ge GM \ge HM$$

Equal only when all observations are equal.

Two positive values$$AM \times HM = GM^2$$

4Which mean?

MeanUse forAgri example

HMRates and reciprocal relationshipsAverage speed of a tractor over equal distances

Raw data: sort first. Discrete data: use cumulative frequencies. Grouped data: find the class, then interpolate.

6Raw data positions

First quartile$$Q_1 = \left(\tfrac{n+1}{4}\right)^{\text{th}}$$
Third quartile$$Q_3 = \left(\tfrac{3(n+1)}{4}\right)^{\text{th}}$$
$d$th decile$$D_d = \left(\tfrac{d(n+1)}{10}\right)^{\text{th}}$$
$p$th percentile$$P_p = \left(\tfrac{p(n+1)}{100}\right)^{\text{th}}$$

7Grouped data (interpolation)

Quartile $Q_1$$$Q_1 = l_1 + \frac{\frac{n}{4} - m_1}{f_1}\, c_1$$
Quartile $Q_3$$$Q_3 = l_3 + \frac{\frac{3n}{4} - m_3}{f_3}\, c_3$$
Decile$$D_d = l + \frac{\frac{dn}{10} - cf}{f}\, c$$
Percentile$$P_p = l + \frac{\frac{pn}{100} - cf}{f}\, c$$

$l$ lower boundary of the class holding the position · $m$ or $cf$ cumulative frequency before it · $f$ its frequency · $c$ its width.

!Common mistakes

  • Forgetting the antilog at the end of a GM.
  • Using GM or HM with zero or negative values.
  • AM for average speed over equal distances (use HM).
  • Finding quartiles before sorting.

Measures of Dispersion

Range, interquartile range, mean absolute deviation, variance, standard deviation and the coefficient of variation.
5-minute revision
Authors: Dr. Pratheesh P. Gopinath · Dr. Adarsh V.S. · Mohammed Hisham M. · Dr. Manju Mary Paul

1What is dispersion?

The extent to which observations deviate from a central value: the spread of the data.

Absolute measures

In the data's units: range, IQR, MAD, SD

Relative measures

Unit-free: coefficient of variation (CV)

A good measure is rigidly defined, easy to compute, based on all values, suitable for further algebra and not unduly affected by extremes.

2Range & IQR

Range$$R = L - S$$
Interquartile range$$IQR = Q_3 - Q_1$$
  • Range: simplest; uses only the two extremes.
  • IQR: spread of the middle 50%; less affected by extremes.
  • Quartile deviation = IQR ÷ 2.

3Mean absolute deviation

MAD about the mean$$MAD = \frac{\sum \lvert x_i - \bar{x} \rvert}{n}$$

Uses all observations but ignores signs. MAD is least about the median.

4Variance & standard deviation

Sample variance$$s^2 = \frac{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2}{n-1}$$
Shortcut$$s^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\left\{\sum x_i^2 - \frac{(\sum x_i)^2}{n}\right\}$$
Discrete frequency$$s^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\left\{\sum f_i x_i^2 - \frac{(\sum f_i x_i)^2}{n}\right\}$$
Grouped, $d_i = (x_i - A)/c$$$s = c\sqrt{\frac{1}{n-1}\left\{\sum f_i d_i^2 - \frac{(\sum f_i d_i)^2}{n}\right\}}$$

SD $s$ = non-negative square root of $s^2$. Grouped data: $x_i$ = class mark, $A$ = class mark with the highest frequency, $c$ = class interval.

5Properties to remember

  • Variance is in squared units; SD is in the original units.
  • SD is always ≥ 0; SD = 0 means all observations are identical.
  • Sample variance uses divisor $n-1$ (unbiased).
  • Adding a constant to every value does not change SD.
  • Multiplying by $b$ multiplies SD by $\lvert b\rvert$.
  • Squaring gives extreme deviations more weight.

6Coefficient of variation

CV (%)$$CV = \frac{s}{\bar{x}} \times 100$$

A relative measure, used to compare variability of datasets in different units or with different means.

Higher CV

More relative variability: less consistent

Lower CV

Less relative variability: more consistent

3Bowley's (quartile) measure

Based on quartiles$$S_Q = \frac{Q_3 - 2Q_2 + Q_1}{Q_3 - Q_1}$$

3Karl Pearson's correlation coefficient $r$

Covariance$$cov(x,y) = \frac{1}{n}\sum (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})$$

1The model

Regression models how a dependent (response) variable depends on one or more independent (predictor) variables.

Simple linear regression$$y_i = \alpha + \beta x_i + e_i$$

Error $e_i$

Theoretical deviation from the true line

Residual $\hat{e}_i$

Observed − fitted: $y_i - (\hat\alpha + \hat\beta x_i)$

2Slope & intercept

  • Slope $\beta$: expected change in $y$ for a one-unit increase in $x$. Range $-\infty$ to $+\infty$; $+$ direct, $-$ inverse.
  • Intercept $\alpha$: value of $y$ when $x = 0$.
Simple1 predictor
Multiple2 or more: $y = \alpha + \beta_1 x_1 + \cdots + \beta_k x_k + e$
LogisticCategorical response

3Least squares

Choose $\alpha, \beta$ to minimise $E = \sum [y_i - (\alpha + \beta x_i)]^2$. This gives the normal equations:

First$$\textstyle\sum y = n\alpha + \beta \sum x$$
Second$$\textstyle\sum xy = \alpha \sum x + \beta \sum x^2$$
Slope$$\hat\beta_{yx} = \frac{\sum xy - \frac{\sum x \sum y}{n}}{\sum x^2 - \frac{(\sum x)^2}{n}}$$
Intercept$$\hat\alpha = \bar{y} - \hat\beta \bar{x}$$

Equivalently $\hat\beta_{yx} = cov(x,y)/var(x)$. The fitted line passes through $(\bar{x}, \bar{y})$.

4Two regression lines

$y$ on $x$ (predict $y$)$$\beta_{yx} = r\frac{\sigma_y}{\sigma_x}$$
$x$ on $y$ (predict $x$)$$\beta_{xy} = r\frac{\sigma_x}{\sigma_y}$$
Link to correlation$$\beta_{yx}\,\beta_{xy} = r^2$$

5Properties of regression coefficients

  • Both have the same sign, and $r$ takes that sign: $r = \pm\sqrt{\beta_{yx}\beta_{xy}}$.
  • If one exceeds 1, the other must be less than 1.
  • $r = 0$: both coefficients are zero.
  • $r = \pm 1$: the two lines coincide.
  • Independent of change of origin, not of scale: with $X=\frac{x-a}{h}$, $Y=\frac{y-b}{k}$, $\beta_{YX} = \frac{h}{k}\beta_{yx}$.

6Assumptions

  • Linearity of the mean relationship
  • Independence of errors (no autocorrelation)
  • Constant error variance: homoscedasticity
  • Normality of errors, for exact inference
  • No exact multicollinearity among predictors
  • Correct model specification

Check the fitted model with residual plots.

1Central Limit Theorem

As $n$ increases$$\frac{\bar{X} - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \xrightarrow{d} N(0, 1)$$
  • Large sample: generally $n \ge 30$.
  • Needs independent observations and finite variance.
  • Standard error = SD of a sampling distribution; for $\bar{x}$ it is $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$, which shrinks as $n$ grows.

2Z test decision rules

State $H_0$ about the population parameter; compute $Z$ from sample statistics.

3Proportions

Single proportion, $H_0: P = P_0$$$Z = \frac{p - P_0}{\sqrt{P_0 Q_0 / n}},\quad Q_0 = 1 - P_0$$
Two proportions, $H_0: P_1 = P_2$$$Z = \frac{p_1 - p_2}{\sqrt{\hat{P}\hat{Q}\left(\frac{1}{n_1} + \frac{1}{n_2}\right)}}$$

Pooled $\hat{P} = \dfrac{n_1 p_1 + n_2 p_2}{n_1 + n_2}$, $\hat{Q} = 1 - \hat{P}$. Needs enough expected successes and failures.

4Single mean, $H_0: \mu = \mu_0$

$\sigma$ known$$Z = \frac{\bar{x} - \mu_0}{\sigma / \sqrt{n}}$$
$\sigma$ unknown$$Z = \frac{\bar{x} - \mu_0}{s / \sqrt{n}}$$

With a large sample, $s$ is a good substitute for $\sigma$.

5Two means, $H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2$

Equal SDs$$Z = \frac{\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2}{\sigma\sqrt{\frac{1}{n_1} + \frac{1}{n_2}}}$$
Pooled $\sigma$ if unknown$$\sigma = \sqrt{\frac{n_1 s_1^2 + n_2 s_2^2}{n_1 + n_2}}$$
Unequal SDs$$Z = \frac{\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2}{\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{\sigma_2^2}{n_2}}}$$

For independent samples, the variance of a difference is the sum of the variances.

6Two SDs

Approximate test$$Z = \frac{s_1 - s_2}{\sqrt{\frac{s_1^2}{2n_1} + \frac{s_2^2}{2n_2}}}$$

Under normal-population assumptions.

1Why design experiments?

Design of Experiments (DoE): planning experiments so treatment effects are estimated accurately, efficiently and without bias.

Objectives: validity · reliability · replicability · optimality.

A good design gives unbiased estimates with their uncertainty, detects important differences, includes the analysis plan, is easy to interpret, has wide validity, uses minimal resources and is as simple as possible.

2Key terms

TreatmentAny object of comparison
ControlStandard treatment for comparison
Experimental unitSmallest unit to which a treatment is independently applied
Observational unitUnit on which the response is measured
ResponseMeasured outcome
Factor / levelsVariable studied / its settings

3Experimental error

Variation among experimental units receiving the same treatment, caused by extraneous factors.

Every design must provide a way to estimate it.

4Fisher's three basic principles

PrincipleWhat it meansWhy
RandomizationTreatments assigned to units purely by chanceProtects against allocation bias; balances unknown sources of variation on average
ReplicationSame treatment on more than one unitEstimates experimental error; improves precision and reliability
Local control (blocking)Group similar units into blocks before randomisingReduces experimental error from known variation

In a Randomized Block Design, all treatments are randomly assigned within each block of similar units.

5Improving precision

  • Border effect: neighbouring plots affect border plants, so record only the inner net plot.
  • Proper plot technique: keep everything except the treatment uniform.
  • Appropriate analysis: e.g. ANCOVA adjusts for covariates.
  • Planning and recording: block for time or batches; keep original records.
  • Matching the design to the experimental units.

6History

  • R.A. Fisher founded the statistical principles of DoE at Rothamsted Experimental Station in the 1920s–30s.
  • He introduced ANOVA to partition variation into factors and chance.
  • With Frank Yates, he built methods still central to agricultural research.
  • Fisher's tea-tasting experiment shows why randomization matters.

1What is a uniformity trial?

A trial to study the nature and extent of fertility variation in a field.

  • One crop variety over the whole field; all inputs applied uniformly.
  • Remove a border, then harvest separately by basic units (smallest recorded unit).
  • Yield differences among basic units measure soil heterogeneity.

Helps decide: plot size, plot shape, block size and blocking direction.

2Fertility contour map

A map of yields over the field. Moving averages smooth random variation; areas of similar fertility are joined by contour lines.

Rice example: 12 m × 17 m field, 1 m border removed → 10 m × 15 m → 150 basic units of 1 m × 1 m.

3Serial correlation

Neighbouring units$$r_s = \frac{\sum X_i X_{i+1} - \frac{(\sum X_i)^2}{n}}{\sum X_i^2 - \frac{(\sum X_i)^2}{n}}$$

Computed horizontally and vertically. High $r_s$: neighbouring yields are similar. Low $r_s$: little linear association at that lag (not proof of patches).

4Mean square between strips

Vertical strips$$MS_V = \frac{\frac{\sum V_i^2}{r} - \frac{G^2}{n}}{c - 1}$$
Horizontal strips$$MS_H = \frac{\frac{\sum H_i^2}{c} - \frac{G^2}{n}}{r - 1}$$

A simpler method with the same aim as serial correlation. The larger mean square shows the direction of greater variation. $n = r \times c$.

5Fairfield Smith's variance law

Variance law$$V_x = \frac{V_1}{x^b}$$
Log form (linear)$$\log V_x = \log V_1 - b\log x$$
Regression through origin$$Y = cX,\quad c = -b$$

$V_x$ = variance of yield per basic unit for plots of $x$ units; $b$ = Smith's index of soil heterogeneity, idealised range 0 to 1. $Y = \log V_x - \log V_1$, $X = \log x$. Example: $b = 0.3607$, adjusted ≈ 0.4116.

6Simulated plots

Variance per unit area$$V_x = \frac{Q_x}{rc - x}$$

Combine basic units into plots of $x$ units ($w = rc/x$ plots, fitting the field exactly). A plot's yield is the sum of its units.

7Maximum curvature method

Plot plot size (X-axis) against coefficient of variation (Y-axis). CV generally falls as plot size rises; the point of maximum curvature (the bend) is taken as the optimum plot size.

Larger plots

Lower CV, but fewer plots fit a fixed area.

Caution

The bend depends on axis scales and the available arrangements: treat it as a guide.

!Common mistakes

  • Different shapes partition the same field, so their variances are dependent: no ordinary F test.
  • High $b$ alone doesn't prove a patch pattern.

1The idea

ANOVA (Analysis of Variance) compares population means by partitioning the total variation into parts due to known sources and to error.

Null hypothesis (one-way)$$H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2 = \cdots = \mu_v$$

A significant $F$ means at least one pair of means differs. It is an omnibus test and doesn't say which pairs.

2Assumptions

  • Observations from normal populations
  • Common variance: homogeneity of variance
  • Samples drawn independently
  • Treatment effects are additive

So errors are iid $N(0, \sigma^2)$. Check with residual plots: residuals vs fitted values, and a normal Q-Q plot.

Error SS$$SS_E = SS_T - SS_{Tr}$$
Mean squares$$MS = \frac{SS}{df}$$
F ratio$$F = \frac{MS_{Tr}}{MSE}$$

$v$ treatments, $n$ observations, $T_i$ treatment totals, $G$ grand total.

1Why a post-hoc test?

A significant ANOVA ($F$) shows that not all means are equal, but not which pairs differ.

Number of pairs$$N = \binom{v}{2} = \frac{v(v-1)}{2}$$
Inflated Type I error$$1 - (1 - \alpha)^N$$

FWER (family-wise error rate): probability of at least one Type I error across the whole family of comparisons.

2Fisher's LSD = Critical Difference (CD)

Equal replications$$\text{LSD} = t_{\alpha/2,\,edf}\sqrt{\frac{2\,MSE}{r}}$$
Unequal: $SE(d)$$$\sqrt{MSE\left(\frac{1}{r_i} + \frac{1}{r_j}\right)}$$

Two steps: significant ANOVA first (protected LSD), then pairs. Means differ if $\lvert \bar{T}_i - \bar{T}_j \rvert > \text{LSD}$. Relatively liberal.

3Duncan's Multiple Range Test (DMRT)

SE of a mean$$SE(\bar{Y}) = \sqrt{\frac{MSE}{r}}$$
Shortest significant range$$R_p = r_p \cdot SE(\bar{Y})$$
  1. Rank the means
  2. Get $SE(\bar Y)$ and table $r_p$
  3. Compare each difference with its $R_p$

$p$ = number of ordered means spanned, including both ends (adjacent: $p = 2$). $R_p$ grows with $p$. Popular in plant sciences.

4Tukey's HSD

Honestly significant difference$$\text{HSD} = q_{\alpha,(v,\,edf)}\sqrt{\frac{MSE}{r}}$$

Uses the studentised range $q$. Compares all pairs while controlling the FWER. One critical value for equal replication. Most conservative of the three; needs no preliminary ANOVA.

CF $= G^2/v^2$. Suited to a modest number of treatments: too few leave little error df, too many need many homogeneous plots.

6Critical difference (CD)

CRD (equal) & RCBD$$t_{\alpha/2,\,edf}\sqrt{\frac{2\,MSE}{r}}$$
Latin square$$t_{\alpha/2,\,edf}\sqrt{\frac{2\,MSE}{v}}$$
CRD (unequal $r_i$, $r_j$)$$t_{\alpha/2,\,edf}\sqrt{MSE\left(\frac{1}{r_i} + \frac{1}{r_j}\right)}$$

Used only after a significant treatment $F$. Means sharing a letter are not significantly different.

7How many replications?

A target of about 12 error df is a practical planning guide, not a rule. Replication should also reflect the power and precision wanted, the expected variability and the available resources.

Correction factor = (grand total)² ÷ total number of observations. Treatment comparisons always use the error mean square.

!Common mistakes

  • Blocks laid along the gradient instead of across it.
  • Using RCBD error df for a CRD.
  • Applying CD when $F$ is not significant.

Factorial Experiments

Main effects, simple effects and interactions; 2ⁿ and 3ⁿ series, Yates' method and confounding.
5-minute revision
Authors: Dr. Pratheesh P. Gopinath · Dr. Adarsh V.S. · Mohammed Hisham M. · Dr. Manju Mary Paul
STAT 3202#KAUMeLONOfficial revision sheetKAU Me-LON · www.agrinoteskau.com · Dept. of Agricultural Statistics, College of Agriculture, Vellayani

1The idea

A factorial experiment studies two or more factors simultaneously, using all combinations of their levels.

Treatment combinations$$N = \prod s_i$$
Series$$2^n,\ 3^n$$

$2^2 = 4$, $2^3 = 8$, $2^4 = 16$, $3^2 = 9$, $3^3 = 27$ combinations. Treatment df = $N - 1$.

2Effects

Main effectAverage change from one factor's level change, over the other factors
Simple effectEffect of one factor at a particular level of another
InteractionEffect of one factor depends on the level of another

Parallel mean profiles = no interaction; non-parallel = possible interaction (needs a test).

3$2^2$ factorial ($r$ replications)

Main effect of A$$\frac{(A)_{i=1} - (A)_{i=0}}{2r}$$
SS due to A$$SS_A = \frac{[(A)_{i=1} - (A)_{i=0}]^2}{4r}$$
A × B interaction$$\frac{(AB)_{i+j=0} - (AB)_{i+j=1}}{2r}$$

A, B and A×B each have 1 df. In $2^3$: divisors become $4r$ (effects) and $8r$ (SS); A, B, C, AB, AC, BC, ABC each have 1 df. Yates' method is the tabular algorithm for $2^n$ effects; Fisher's algebraic method is the alternative.

4Degrees of freedom

Factor with $a$ levels$a - 1$
A × B$(a-1)(b-1)$
$3^2$: A, B2 each
$3^2$: A × B4

A 3-level factor's 2 df split into linear $A_L = A_2 - A_0$ and quadratic $A_Q = A_2 + A_0 - 2A_1$.

5Types of factorials

SymmetricalAll factors have the same number of levels ($2^n$, $3^n$)
AsymmetricalUnequal levels, e.g. $2 \times 3$
Complete (full)All combinations included
FractionalOnly a selected fraction of combinations

6Critical differences

Interaction means ($2^2$; ABC in $2^3$)$$t\sqrt{\frac{2\,MSE}{r}}$$
Two-factor means in $2^3$$$t\sqrt{\frac{MSE}{r}}$$
Levels of A in $3^2$$$t\sqrt{\frac{2\,MSE}{3r}}$$
Levels of B in $2 \times 3$$$t\sqrt{\frac{2\,MSE}{2r}}$$

$t = t_{\alpha/2,\,edf}$. If the interaction is significant, compare combination means, not main-effect means.

7Confounding

Deliberately making an interaction inseparable from block effects so that blocks can be smaller.

Complete

The same interaction is confounded in every replication.

Partial

Different interactions confounded in different replications.

Gainsmaller, more homogeneous blocks and better precision · Costlost information on the confounded effect. Higher-order interactions are usually the ones confounded.

!Common mistakes

  • Interpreting main effects when the interaction is significant.
  • Mixing up $2r$ (effect) and $4r$ (SS) divisors in $2^2$.
  • Confounding a main effect.

  4. If the response to nitrogen changes with the irrigation level, the two factors show .interaction

Analysis of Covariance

Combining ANOVA with regression: covariates, the common-slope model, adjusted means and the adjusted F test.
5-minute revision
Authors: Dr. Pratheesh P. Gopinath · Dr. Adarsh V.S. · Mohammed Hisham M. · Dr. Manju Mary Paul
STAT 3202#KAUMeLONOfficial revision sheetKAU Me-LON · www.agrinoteskau.com · Dept. of Agricultural Statistics, College of Agriculture, Vellayani

1What is ANCOVA?

ANCOVA combines analysis of variance with regression adjustment on a covariate: a measured explanatory variable.

Agri examples: initial plant stand, initial weight of animals, pre-treatment pest count, previous-season yield.

A covariate must be related to the response and not caused by the treatment. Ideally measured before treatment (pre-treatment covariate).

2Blocking vs ANCOVA

BlockingANCOVA
WhenBefore the experiment (layout)During analysis
HandlesPredictable variation (soil, gradient)Variation in a measured covariate
AimBoth reduce experimental error

Precision may improve but is not guaranteed.

3Model & key quantities (CRD)

Common-slope model$$Y_{ij} = \mu + \tau_i + \beta(X_{ij} - \bar{X}) + e_{ij}$$
Slope$$\hat\beta = \frac{E_{xy}}{E_{xx}}$$
Adjusted error SS$$E = E_{yy} - \frac{E_{xy}^2}{E_{xx}}$$
Adjusted MSE$$MSE_{adj} = \frac{E}{n - v - 1}$$

$E_{xx}, E_{xy}, E_{yy}$ = within-treatment (error) sums of squares and products. RBD error df: $(r-1)(v-1) - 1$.

4Degrees of freedom

Fitting one covariate slope uses one error df.

CRD$n - v - 1$
RBD$(r-1)(v-1) - 1$

5Adjusted means & F test

Adjusted treatment mean$$\bar{Y}_{i,adj} = \bar{Y}_i - \hat\beta(\bar{X}_i - \bar{X})$$
Adjusted F$$F = \frac{(E_1 - E)/(v - 1)}{MSE_{adj}}$$

$E_1$: residual SS of the reduced model (no treatments, covariate kept); $E$: full model. Means are compared at a common covariate value.

6Assumptions

  • The usual ANOVA assumptions: independent errors, common variance, normality for exact tests
  • Linear relationship between response and covariate
  • Equal slopes across treatments (check treatment × covariate interaction)
  • Covariate not affected by the treatments
  • Error mean is zero

7Steps of the analysis

  1. Compute SS for $X$, $Y$ and SP for $XY$
  2. Get error $E_{xx}, E_{xy}, E_{yy}$
  3. Estimate $\hat\beta = E_{xy}/E_{xx}$
  4. Adjusted error SS and $MSE_{adj}$
  5. Adjusted treatment SS: $E_1 - E$
  6. Adjusted $F$ test
  7. Compute adjusted means
  8. Compare with pair-specific SEs

!Common mistakes

  • Using a covariate the treatment affects.
  • Forgetting the extra df lost.
  • Assuming adjustment turns observational data into an experiment.

Missing Plot Analysis

Handling a lost observation in CRD, RCBD and Latin square: direct fitting, Bartlett's covariance method and substitution.
5-minute revision
Authors: Dr. Pratheesh P. Gopinath · Dr. Adarsh V.S. · Mohammed Hisham M. · Dr. Manju Mary Paul
STAT 3202#KAUMeLONOfficial revision sheetKAU Me-LON · www.agrinoteskau.com · Dept. of Agricultural Statistics, College of Agriculture, Vellayani

1Why plots go missing

Animal or pest damage, flooding, theft, recording errors… Always record why an observation is missing.

A poor result caused by a treatment is real data and must not be discarded.

A missing plot destroys the balance of an RCBD or Latin square; a CRD copes directly.

2CRD: fit directly

Analyse the observed responses with unequal treatment sample sizes (no substitution needed).

Error df$$edf = n_{obs} - v$$
SE of a difference$$SE = \sqrt{MSE\left(\frac{1}{n_i} + \frac{1}{n_j}\right)}$$

3RCBD: one missing value

Substitution estimate$$\hat{x} = \frac{rB + vT - G}{(r-1)(v-1)}$$

$B$, $T$ = observed totals of the affected block and treatment; $G$ = observed grand total (all excluding the missing value).

Error df$$(r-1)(v-1) - 1$$

4Latin square: one missing value

Substitution estimate$$\hat{x} = \frac{v(R + C + T) - 2G}{(v-1)(v-2)}$$

$R$, $C$, $T$ = observed totals of the affected row, column and treatment; $G$ = observed grand total.

Error df$$(v-1)(v-2) - 1$$

5Bartlett's covariance method

Create an imaginary covariate $X$: 1 (or −1) for the missing plot, 0 elsewhere. Set $Y = 0$ for the missing plot. Then run ANCOVA for the design.

The indicator accounts for the missing position: total and error df are each one less than in the complete design.

6After substituting

  • The estimate minimises the error SS.
  • Subtract one error df per estimated value.
  • The treatment SS can be inflated: apply the bias correction.
  • Comparisons involving the affected treatment need adjusted SEs.
  • Several missing values: estimate by iteration.

7Choosing an approach

DesignApproachError df
CRDFit observed data (unequal $n_i$)$n_{obs} - v$
RCBDSubstitution $\hat x$, Bartlett's method, or fit the observed data$(r-1)(v-1) - 1$
Latin squareSubstitution $\hat x$ or Bartlett's method$(v-1)(v-2) - 1$

!Common mistakes

  • Counting $\hat x$ as a real observation.
  • Including the missing cell in $B$, $T$ or $G$.
  • Ignoring missingness linked to the response itself (bias).

Data Transformation

When ANOVA assumptions fail: log, square root, angular and Box-Cox transformations, and back-transformation.
5-minute revision
Authors: Dr. Pratheesh P. Gopinath · Dr. Adarsh V.S. · Mohammed Hisham M. · Dr. Manju Mary Paul
STAT 3202#KAUMeLONOfficial revision sheetKAU Me-LON · www.agrinoteskau.com · Dept. of Agricultural Statistics, College of Agriculture, Vellayani

1Why transform?

Used when ANOVA assumptions fail, typically unequal error variances (heteroscedasticity), non-normal errors or non-additive (multiplicative) effects.

  1. Transform the data
  2. Run ANOVA and mean separation on the transformed scale
  3. Back-transform means to the original units for reporting

Never pick a transformation just to get the conclusion you want.

2Which transformation?

DataVarianceUse
Counts (Poisson)$\sigma^2 = \mu$ or $c^2\mu$Square root
Binomial percentages$\sigma^2 = p(1-p)/n$Angular (arcsine)
SD ∝ mean; multiplicative effects$\sigma^2 = c^2\mu^2$Logarithmic

3Logarithmic

Positive data$$X = \log Y$$
With zeros$$X = \log(Y + 1)$$

Back-transform: $Y = \text{antilog}\,X$. Logs need positive inputs. Example in the book: weed counts.

4Square root

Counts$$X = \sqrt{Y}$$
Zeros present$$X = \sqrt{Y + 1}$$

Very small counts: $\sqrt{Y} + \sqrt{Y+1}$. Back-transform $\sqrt{Y+1}$ with $Y = X^2 - 1$. Insect, larval, defective and diseased-seed counts.

5Angular (arcsine) transformation

Transform$$X = \sin^{-1}\sqrt{p},\quad p = \frac{Y}{100}$$
0% and 100%$$p = \frac{1}{4n},\quad p = 1 - \frac{1}{4n}$$
Back-transform$$Y = 100 \sin^2 X$$

For percentages from binomial counts (germination, mortality). The 30–70% guideline is approximate. Nutrient percentages (protein, carbohydrate) are not binomial and need not be transformed.

6Box-Cox

A family of power transformations indexed by $\lambda$, for strictly positive data.

$\lambda = \tfrac12$: square root · $\lambda = -1$: reciprocal · $\lambda \to 0$: log. The angular transformation is not in this family.

7After transforming

  • Check assumptions using the residuals of the fitted model.
  • Use one transformation consistently for the whole dataset.
  • CD on the transformed scale: $t_{\alpha/2,\,edf}\sqrt{2\,MSE/r}$ (RBD).
  • Back-transformed means are not arithmetic means (e.g. squared root means are ≤ the original mean).

!Common mistakes

  • Reporting transformed means as final results.
  • Taking log of zero.
  • Arcsine on non-binomial percentages.
