Hypothesis Testing

Null and alternative hypotheses, Type I and II errors, significance and power, critical regions, tails and p-values.

THE IDEA TO KEEP

State the hypotheses before looking at the test result.

RememberA p-value is a probability under the null model, not the probability that the null hypothesis is true.

Apply itAt α = 0.05, p = 0.03 leads to rejection of H₀. A non-significant result means insufficient evidence, not proof of equality.

1 minute: key idea · 3 minutes: methods & examples · 1 minute: test yourself

1Hypotheses

A statistical hypothesis is a testable statement about a population parameter or distribution.

$H_0$: null

No effect, no difference, no relationship. The one tested for possible rejection.

$H_1$ / $H_a$: alternative

An effect, difference or relationship. Decides one- or two-tailed.

Karl Popper's falsification principle: a scientific hypothesis must be testable and potentially falsifiable.

2Errors in decisions

Decision$H_0$ true$H_0$ false
Reject $H_0$Type I error ($\alpha$)Correct: power $1-\beta$
Fail to rejectCorrect ($1-\alpha$)Type II error ($\beta$)
Level of significance$$P(\text{reject } H_0 \mid H_0 \text{ true}) \le \alpha$$
Power$$1 - \beta$$

Larger samples generally increase power. $\alpha$ is fixed before the test (usually 0.05 or 0.01).

3Regions & tails

Test statistic ($Z$, $t$, $\chi^2$, $F$) → compared with its sampling distribution under $H_0$. The critical value separates the rejection (critical) region from the acceptance region.

$H_1$TestReject $H_0$ when$\alpha$ in tail
$\mu \ne \mu_0$Two-tailedStatistic below the lower or above the upper critical value$\alpha/2$ each
$\mu > \mu_0$Right-tailedStatistic > critical value$\alpha$
$\mu < \mu_0$Left-tailedStatistic < critical value$\alpha$

4The p-value

Probability, assuming $H_0$ true, of a statistic as extreme or more extreme than observed.

p ≤ αReject $H_0$
p > αFail to reject $H_0$

Small p = strong evidence against $H_0$.

5The eight steps of a test

  1. State $H_0$
  2. State $H_1$
  3. Set the significance level $\alpha$
  4. Collect the data
  5. Calculate the test statistic
  6. Find the critical value or region
  7. Compare the statistic with it
  8. Make and state the decision

6History

Fisher's tea-tasting experiment: a lady claimed she could tell whether milk or tea was poured first. It's the classic illustration of significance testing.

!Common mistakes

  • Saying "accept $H_0$": say fail to reject · Failing to reject does not prove $H_0$ true · Choosing $\alpha$ after seeing the data · Picking the tail from the result instead of from $H_1$.
